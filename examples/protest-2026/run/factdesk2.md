## 4. Residence permits after a protest arrest without conviction
- **Law (VERIFIED):** the immigration code (CESEDA) allows a permit to be refused or withdrawn for a "threat to public order" under L.432-1 and L.432-4. Since the 2024 law it also allows this for breaching the *contrat d'engagement républicain* (a pledge to respect republican principles) under L.412-9. A conviction is **not legally required**. Case law treats an isolated act as possibly insufficient (Défenseur des droits decision 2023-159). La Cimade criticises how often the 2024 law invokes "public order threat". https://juridique.defenseurdesdroits.fr/doc_num.php?explnum_id=22047 · https://www.lacimade.org/analyse/projet-de-loi-asile-et-immigration-2023/
- **Frequency: UNKNOWN.** I found **no data**, and no report from La Cimade, GISTI or the Défenseur des droits that counts withdrawals tied to protests. The only practitioner commentary comes from a single commercial law firm (Kohen), which says a mere arrest without prosecution "does not characterise a threat" and that a refusal on that basis alone is fragile in court. It also notes that the préfecture can consult the TAJ police records file, so even an arrest with no charge can appear in the file the préfecture reads (ALLEGED, one self-interested source). https://kohenavocats.com/interpelle-relache-manifestation-paris-titre-sejour-oqtf-recours-2026/
- **Bottom line:** the legal exposure is real for **non-EU** residents, and it rises with a TAJ entry or an OQTF (an order to leave French territory) given "sans délai", meaning with no time allowed to leave voluntarily. How often it happens in practice is undocumented. EU citizens fall under a separate, stricter removal standard, which this search did not cover.
